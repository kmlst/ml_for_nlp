\begin{abstract}
This report compares how \fomc{} statements and minutes discuss the two sides of the
Federal Reserve's mandate: inflation and the labor market. The corpus contains 199
meetings from 2000 to 2024, with one statement and one set of minutes for each meeting.
The main measure is a transparent dictionary balance: inflation mentions minus
labor-market mentions, normalized by document length. Statements are shorter, more
concentrated, and more inflation-heavy than minutes. Minutes are longer and closer to
balance, with more space for labor-market conditions, financial stress, and uncertainty.
I then audit the dictionary at the sentence level. A coverage-enriched sample of 3,000
sentences is labelled with a local language model and used to train simple classifiers
based on \tfidf{} and local embeddings. Conditional on these weak labels, both trained
classifiers outperform the dictionary rule by a large margin, with \tfidf{} slightly ahead
of embeddings. The sentence model mainly reveals what the dictionary leaves out:
financial-conditions language, especially in minutes.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The \fed{} has a dual mandate: price stability and maximum employment. After each
\fomc{} meeting, the Committee publishes a short statement. A few weeks later, it
publishes longer minutes. Both documents refer to the same meeting, but they serve
different communication roles. The statement gives the immediate public message; the
minutes give a fuller account of the discussion.

This report asks whether the two documents give the same relative attention to inflation
and the labor market:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Do statements and minutes give the same relative attention to inflation and the labor market? Do they discuss the two subjects in the same way?}
\end{quote}

The question matters because the mandate combines two objectives, while the communication
format may emphasize them differently. Price stability is closely tied to credibility,
inflation expectations, and the transmission of monetary policy. Labor-market language
can instead describe slack, wage pressure, resilience, or recession risk. The same
meeting can therefore produce a short statement centered on inflation and longer minutes
that give more room to employment and financial conditions.

The report takes a macro-topic angle: inflation versus the labor market. Mathieu's
companion report studies the related policy-tone angle: hawkish versus dovish
communication. The two views overlap, but the mapping is imperfect. Inflation language
can support a hawkish signal, while labor-market language can describe wage pressure or
resilient demand under a restrictive stance.

The empirical strategy starts with a document-level dictionary balance and then checks it
at several levels: term composition, sentence matches, low-rank \tfidf{} projections,
rate-decision groups, policy periods, and election windows. I then use LLM-assisted weak
labels to train sentence classifiers with \tfidf{} and embedding features. These methods
serve as checks on the same contrast: inflation language versus labor-market language.

\section*{Related Work}
\label{sec:literature}

A large literature studies \fomc{} communication through its effect on financial markets,
especially fixed-income instruments and Overnight Indexed Swap rates, which closely
track market expectations of monetary policy. Tadle \citep{tadle2022minutes} builds
sentiment indices for statements and minutes and relates minutes sentiment to
financial-market reactions. This report stays on the textual side and does not estimate
market responses.

Several papers use text methods to study \fomc{} communication directly. Acosta and
Meade \citep{acosta2015hanging} study post-meeting statements with semantic similarity
methods and document how statements changed over time. Hansen, McMahon, and Prat
\citep{hansen2018transparency} apply computational linguistics to internal \fomc{}
transcripts to study deliberation and transparency inside the Committee. Their data are
richer than mine because transcripts contain individual interventions. This report uses
public statements and minutes, so it measures released communication rather than internal
debate.

The closest paper for the dual-mandate motivation is Shapiro and Wilson
\citep{shapiro2022taking}. They estimate central-bank objectives from the language of
internal meetings, including the relative weight placed on inflation and real activity.
My report is narrower: it describes how inflation and labor-market topics appear in
public communication, without estimating the economic weight of each objective.

Doh, Kim, and Yang \citep{doh2021matters} show that qualitative descriptions in
\fomc{} statements can matter for bond prices. Doh, Song, and Yang
\citep{doh2025deciphering} use alternative \fomc{} statements and language models to
measure hawkish and dovish policy stance. Those papers are closer to the companion report
on policy tone.

\section*{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The corpus comes from the official \fed{} website and is retrieved and parsed
automatically. Each row corresponds to one meeting and contains the meeting date, the
statement URL, the minutes URL, the statement text, and the minutes text.

The sample contains 199 meetings from February 2000 to December 2024. Every meeting has
both a statement and minutes. Rate decisions are inferred from statement text with simple
regular expressions and coded as \emph{hike}, \emph{cut}, or \emph{hold}. The sample has
59 hikes, 30 cuts, and 110 holds.

Minutes contain about fourteen times more words than statements on average. All dictionary
scores are therefore reported as mentions per 1,000 words. Preprocessing is deliberately
minimal: texts are lowercased, whitespace is normalized, and words are counted with a
regular expression. Stopwords are kept because several dictionary entries are multi-word
expressions, such as \emph{price stability}, \emph{labor market}, and \emph{inflation
expectations}.

\subsection*{Dictionary balance}

I define two short dictionaries. The inflation dictionary contains terms such as
\emph{inflation}, \emph{inflationary}, \emph{price stability}, \emph{price pressures},
\emph{inflation expectations}, \emph{core inflation}, \emph{PCE inflation},
\emph{consumer prices}, \emph{energy prices}, \emph{food prices}, \emph{cost pressures},
\emph{supply constraints}, and \emph{disinflation}.

The labor-market dictionary contains terms such as \emph{employment},
\emph{unemployment}, \emph{labor market}, \emph{job gains}, \emph{payrolls},
\emph{hiring}, \emph{layoffs}, \emph{wages}, \emph{labor demand}, \emph{labor supply},
\emph{labor force}, \emph{slack}, \emph{participation}, \emph{vacancies}, and
\emph{job openings}.

For a document $d$, the two attention scores are:

\begin{equation}
    I(d) = 1000 \times
    \frac{\#\{\text{inflation terms in } d\}}
    {\#\{\text{words in } d\}},
    \qquad
    L(d) = 1000 \times
    \frac{\#\{\text{labor-market terms in } d\}}
    {\#\{\text{words in } d\}}.
\end{equation}

The balance score is:

\begin{equation}
    B(d) = I(d) - L(d).
    \label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}

A positive value means that inflation terms are more frequent than labor-market terms.
The score is a coarse measure of relative textual attention. Its limits are mechanical:
phrases can overlap with single words, and words such as \emph{wages} can appear in
labor-market descriptions as well as inflation-pressure narratives. Because this score is
the main measure, I audit it through term counts and sentence-level matches.

The sentence check tags each sentence as \emph{inflation only}, \emph{labor only},
\emph{both}, or \emph{neither}, depending on exact dictionary matches. I also build a
low-rank \tfidf{} representation of statements and minutes. After vectorizing documents
with unigrams and bigrams, I apply truncated singular value decomposition
$X = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^\top$. The first components capture the largest directions of
variation in the \tfidf{} matrix. If statements and minutes use systematically different
vocabularies, formulas, or recurring expressions, this format effect should appear in the
first dimensions.

Inflation language is more frequent than labor-market language in both document types,

message. Minutes cover more background discussion, so the same macro topics take up a
smaller share of the text. Labor-market vocabulary also becomes more frequent over the

\section*{Sentence-Level Weak Labels and Topic Classification}
\label{sec:sentence-classifier}

The dictionary score is transparent, but narrow. Many important \fomc{} sentences use
neither inflation nor labor-market vocabulary. During stress periods, for example,
communication often turns to credit, market functioning, liquidity, asset prices, or
financial stress. These sentences can be central to the meeting narrative while being
classified as \emph{neither} by the inflation--labor dictionary.

I therefore add a broader sentence-level audit. The task asks whether a sentence can be
assigned to one of five broad \fomc{} topics: inflation, labor, financial conditions,
policy decision, or other. This gives a supervised NLP component while keeping the
dictionary balance as the main interpretable document measure.

\subsection*{Sentence table and weak annotation}

I split all statements and minutes into sentences with a punctuation-based rule and drop
very short sentences. This gives 58,976 usable sentences. For each sentence, I keep the
meeting date, document type, sentence position, rate decision, period, calendar
indicators, document-level dictionary balance, and sentence-level dictionary label.

I then construct a coverage-enriched sample of 3,000 sentences for annotation. The sample
covers statements and minutes, the four dictionary sentence buckets, meetings with large
statement--minutes gaps, crisis periods, the post-Covid inflation period, and federal
election windows. Remaining slots are filled with additional sampled sentences. This
sampling design is useful for auditing the dictionary, but raw label shares in the
annotated sample should not be interpreted as corpus frequencies.

The 3,000 sentences are labelled with a local language model, \code{llama3.2}, using a
fixed JSON schema. The main label is \emph{main\_topic}, with six possible values:
\emph{inflation}, \emph{labor}, \emph{both}, \emph{financial\_conditions},
\emph{policy\_decision}, and \emph{other}. The prompt asks the model to classify only
from the sentence itself. Sentences about credit, markets, liquidity, volatility, asset
prices, or market functioning are assigned to \emph{financial\_conditions}.

The annotations are weak labels generated under a fixed topic schema. The classifier
results below measure agreement with this schema rather than accuracy against a manually
validated benchmark.

\reportfigure{figures/dictionary_llm_sentence_confusion.png}{0.82}
{Dictionary sentence labels compared with LLM-assisted sentence topics. The main gap is that many dictionary-\emph{other} sentences are labelled as financial conditions.}

The comparison between dictionary labels and LLM-assisted labels identifies the main
blind spot of the dictionary. Many sentences with inflation terms are labelled as
inflation or both, and many sentences with labor-market terms are labelled as labor or
both. The main disagreement comes from dictionary-\emph{other} sentences labelled as
\emph{financial\_conditions}. Financial-stress language often matters for the meeting
narrative without using either inflation or labor-market terms.

\subsection*{Sentence classifiers}

The supervised input is one sentence. The target is the LLM-assisted \emph{main\_topic}
label. For evaluation, I merge \emph{policy\_decision} and \emph{other} into a single
\emph{other\_or\_policy} class, because both are non-mandate categories and the smaller
class would make the comparison noisier. The final target classes are therefore:
\emph{inflation}, \emph{labor}, \emph{both}, \emph{financial\_conditions}, and
\emph{other\_or\_policy}.

I compare four ways of assigning a topic to a sentence:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item The \emph{dictionary rule} maps the sentence-level dictionary label directly
    into the supervised classes. It has no training step and serves as the transparent
    baseline.
    \item The \emph{embedding prototype} baseline embeds short descriptions of each
    topic and assigns each sentence to the nearest topic description. It uses embeddings,
    but no supervised training.
    \item The \tfidf{} model represents each sentence with unigram and bigram \tfidf{}
    features and trains a balanced logistic regression.
    \item The embedding model represents each sentence with a local \code{nomic-embed-text}
    vector and trains the same balanced logistic regression on dense features.
\end{enumerate}

The two logistic-regression models are evaluated with stratified cross-validation on the
weakly labelled sample. Macro-F1 is the main metric because the classes are unevenly
distributed and minority topics matter for interpretation.

Once the weak-label schema is fixed, both supervised models recover the assigned sentence
topics much better than the dictionary rule and the no-training prototype baseline. The
\tfidf{} model is slightly ahead of the embedding model. This fits the corpus: many
\fomc{} topics have stable phrases, and sparse unigram--bigram features capture them
well. The embedding model remains useful as a compact semantic representation, especially
when related sentences use different surface words.

\subsection*{Full-corpus sentence topics}

After cross-validation, I fit a sentence-topic classifier on the annotated sample and
apply it to all 58,976 sentences. In the notebook run used for the figures, full
sentence embeddings are available, so the full-corpus predictions use the embedding
logistic-regression model. If full embeddings are unavailable, the notebook falls back to
the \tfidf{} classifier. The resulting file
\code{data/processed/fomc\_sentence\_topic\_predictions.csv} contains one row per
sentence, with predicted topic, confidence, meeting date, document type, period, and
calendar variables.

The cross-validation table evaluates held-out agreement with the weak labels. The
full-corpus prediction uses the fitted classifier as a descriptive measurement device.
The most reliable comparisons are relative: statements versus minutes, crisis versus
non-crisis periods, and dictionary balance versus classifier balance.

\reportfigure{figures/embedding_topic_shares_by_document_type.png}{0.78}
{Predicted sentence-topic shares for statements and minutes. The classifier view keeps the inflation--labor contrast but adds financial conditions and policy-decision language.}

\reportfigure{figures/embedding_topic_shares_over_time.png}{0.82}
{Predicted sentence-topic shares over time. See peak around 2008 for financial condition.}

\reportfigure{figures/dictionary_vs_embedding_balance.png}{0.72}
{Dictionary inflation-minus-labor balance against the classifier inflation-minus-labor sentence share. Each point is a meeting-document pair.}

The classifier assigns a larger financial-conditions share to minutes than to statements,
and a larger combined-mandate share to statements than to minutes. This matches the
format interpretation from the dictionary and SVD checks. Statements compress the public
message into a short text; minutes contain more background discussion, including
financial conditions.

The scatterplot links the two approaches. The dictionary balance measures exact
inflation terms minus exact labor-market terms per 1,000 words. The classifier balance
measures the predicted share of inflation sentences minus the predicted share of labor
sentences. The two measures broadly move together. Their disagreements help identify
documents where financial-conditions or policy-decision sentences occupy space that the
dictionary leaves outside the mandate balance.

and Chair appointment or reappointment years. These variables are descriptive markers
for scheduled institutional and electoral dates.

\section*{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The main result is a document-format effect. Statements are short and more
inflation-heavy. Minutes are longer, more diffuse, and closer to balance. The
statement--minutes gap shows how much the immediate policy message differs from the
longer meeting account.

The checks point in the same direction. The dictionary score is higher in statements.
The term audit shows that the counts are driven by economically meaningful expressions.
The sentence-level dictionary audit shows how statements compress mandate language. The
low-rank \tfidf{} map shows that statements and minutes occupy different textual regions.
The weak-label classifier adds one more piece: broader topics, especially financial
conditions, can be recovered from sentence text even when exact mandate terms are absent.
The convergence of these checks makes a pure dictionary artifact less likely.

The pattern is consistent with the communication role of statements. A short policy
statement has to foreground the rationale for the decision, and inflation is closely tied
to credibility, expectations, and the transmission of monetary policy. Minutes include
more of the discussion around risks, uncertainty, credit, market functioning, and labor
conditions.

The sentence-level results also clarify the limits of a pure dual-mandate dictionary. In
stress periods, many important sentences are about financial conditions, liquidity, and
market functioning. These sentences belong to \fomc{} communication, but they are outside
the narrow inflation--labor contrast. The classifier view therefore adds a third
dimension that explains part of what the dictionary leaves out.

Rate-decision and period comparisons move in plausible directions. Hike meetings are
more inflation-heavy than cut meetings, and the Covid shock is one of the clearest cases
where labor-market language becomes more visible. Given the small sample and serial
dependence between meetings, these comparisons should be read as descriptive
associations. The election-window check is also descriptive and mostly negative: topic
mix inside and outside 90-day federal election windows looks very similar.

The implication is that document choice matters. A study based only on statements will
mechanically emphasize a different part of the mandate than a study based on minutes.
Models that pool both formats can easily learn format differences before learning a
substantive inflation--labor contrast.

\section*{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this sample of 199 \fomc{} meetings, statements and minutes give different textual
weights to the two sides of the dual mandate. Statements are much shorter and more
inflation-dominant. Minutes are more balanced and contain a wider discussion. This is the
main descriptive result of the report.

Several checks support this result. The dictionary balance is higher in statements. The
term audit shows that the score is driven by meaningful monetary-policy expressions,
although the word \emph{inflation} plays a large role. The sentence-level dictionary audit
shows that statements compress inflation and labor-market language into a short document.
The low-rank \tfidf{} map shows that statements and minutes are different textual
formats. The weak-label sentence classifier shows that broader sentence topics are
learnable from text, with \tfidf{} and embedding features far ahead of the dictionary
rule on the weak-label benchmark.

The inflation--labor contrast captures only part of \fomc{} communication. In stress
periods, financial conditions become central, and a pure dual-mandate dictionary misses
that part of the story. The classifier view makes this visible, especially in minutes.
Its absolute topic shares should be read cautiously because the model is trained on a
small weakly labelled sample, but its relative comparisons help interpret where the
dictionary balance succeeds and where it is incomplete.

These results are descriptive NLP evidence, not an assessment of the Federal Reserve's
true mandate weights. The measures developed here could be used in a broader study of
monetary-policy communication, but they would need to be combined with policy decisions,
macroeconomic conditions, and market reactions before supporting claims about actual
policy preferences.

A small \tfidf{} classification exercise remains in the appendix. The target is either
the current rate decision or the next meeting's rate decision. The models are standard

The rate-decision scores remain modest. The majority baseline has macro-F1 0.222. For
the current decision, the best model is \tfidf{}+\svm{} on full minutes, with macro-F1

0.396. This appendix check suggests that minutes contain more decision-related context
than statements, even though statements have a higher density of mandate terms.
