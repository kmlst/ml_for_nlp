\begin{abstract}
This report studies how \fomc{} statements and minutes differ in their treatment of the
two sides of the Federal Reserve's mandate: inflation and the labor market. We use 199
meetings from 2000 to 2024, with one statement and one set of minutes for each meeting.
The starting point is a transparent dictionary score: inflation language minus
labor-market language, normalized by document length. The main pattern is clear:
statements are short, concentrated, and much more inflation-focused than minutes, while
minutes are longer and closer to balance, with more space for labor-market conditions,
uncertainty, and financial stress. We then split the corpus into sentences, label a
balanced sample with a local language model, and train simple \tfidf{} and embedding
classifiers on those sentence pseudo-labels. The sentence model gives a richer picture:
it confirms the inflation--labor contrast, shows where exact dictionary matching misses
financial-conditions language, and makes embeddings a substantive part of the analysis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The \fed{} has a dual mandate: price stability and maximum employment. After each
\fomc{} meeting, the Committee publishes a short statement. A few weeks later, it
publishes longer minutes. Both documents refer to the same meeting, but they play
different communication roles. The statement is the immediate public message; the minutes
give a more detailed account of the discussion.

This report asks a simple question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Do statements and minutes give the same relative attention to inflation and the labor market? Do they treat both subjects the same way?}
\end{quote}

The report follows the public communication itself: the topics that appear in the
documents, the space given to each side of the mandate, and the way this changes across
document types and periods. The first measure is intentionally transparent. I count how
often each document uses a small set of inflation terms and labor-market terms, then
compare the two counts after normalizing by document length.

The asymmetry is interesting because it is partly expected. The Fed's legal mandate is
dual, but price stability has a special place in central-bank communication. Inflation is
directly tied to credibility, expectations, and the policy instrument. Labor-market
conditions matter throughout the corpus, but they often appear as evidence about slack,
demand, wage pressure, or the cost of tightening. A document can therefore be highly
focused on inflation while still mentioning employment. Conversely, a document can
discuss the labor market while still supporting a restrictive policy stance.

The companion report by Mathieu takes the symmetric policy-tone angle: hawkish versus
dovish communication. This report takes the macro-topic angle: inflation versus the
labor market. The two views complement each other. Inflation language often supports a
hawkish signal, but the mapping is imperfect; labor-market language can describe slack,
wage pressure, or resilience while the overall policy stance remains restrictive.

The report has three parts. First, I compare statements and minutes with the dictionary
score across document types, time, policy episodes, and rate decisions. This gives the
main descriptive result: statements are consistently more inflation-heavy than minutes.
Second, I move to sentences. The corpus contains almost 59,000 usable sentences; a
balanced sample is labelled automatically, then used to train small \tfidf{} and local
embedding classifiers. This step uses embeddings as semantic features in a prediction
task. Third, I apply the sentence classifier to the full corpus and add a small calendar
check around elections and institutional timing. The methods stay close to the style of
the project: pandas, sklearn, simple dictionaries, and figures that can be read directly.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:literature}

This project is part of the broader text-as-data approach to central bank communication.
Blinder et al. \citep{blinder2008central} emphasize that communication is itself a policy
instrument. Central banks move interest rates, explain their decisions, and shape
expectations.

Several papers apply text methods to \fomc{} communication. Acosta and Meade
\citep{acosta2015hanging} study post-meeting statements with semantic similarity tools
and document how statements changed over time. Hansen, McMahon, and Prat
\citep{hansen2018transparency} use internal \fomc{} transcripts to study deliberation
inside the Committee. Their data are richer than mine because transcripts contain
individual interventions, while this report uses public statements and minutes.

The dual-mandate motivation is closest to Shapiro and Wilson
\citep{shapiro2022taking}, who estimate central bank objectives from internal meeting
language. This report uses public statements and minutes, and measures the relative
textual weight of two families of terms.

The statement--minutes comparison is also related to work on the policy signal contained
in central bank wording. Tadle \citep{tadle2022minutes} builds sentiment indices for
\fomc{} statements and minutes and studies their relation with financial markets. Doh,
Kim, and Yang \citep{doh2021matters} show that qualitative descriptions in \fomc{}
statements can move bond prices. Doh, Song, and Yang \citep{doh2025deciphering} use
alternative \fomc{} statements and language models to measure hawkish and dovish stance.
Those papers are closer to the companion hawkish/dovish report. Here, the target is
different: I measure whether the text emphasizes inflation or the labor market.

The choice of a small dictionary also follows a standard warning in text analysis.
Gentzkow, Kelly, and Taddy \citep{gentzkow2019text} stress that a text measure should be
clear about what it measures. Loughran and McDonald \citep{loughran2011liability} show
that generic sentiment dictionaries can be misleading in financial texts. I therefore
use a short dictionary tied to the Fed's dual mandate and to the vocabulary of the
corpus.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The corpus comes from the official \fed{} website and is stored in
\code{data/raw/fomc\_documents\_raw.csv}. Each row corresponds to one meeting and contains
the meeting date, URLs, statement text, and minutes text.

The sample contains 199 meetings from February 2000 to December 2024. Every meeting has
both a statement and minutes. Rate decisions are inferred from statement text with simple
regular expressions and coded as \emph{hike}, \emph{cut}, or \emph{hold}. The sample has
59 hikes, 30 cuts, and 110 holds.

\section{Measures and Checks}
\label{sec:measure}

\subsection{Dictionary balance}

I define two short dictionaries. The inflation dictionary contains terms such as
\emph{inflation}, \emph{inflationary}, \emph{price stability}, \emph{price pressures},
\emph{inflation expectations}, \emph{core inflation}, \emph{PCE inflation},
\emph{consumer prices}, \emph{energy prices}, \emph{food prices}, \emph{cost pressures},
\emph{supply constraints}, and \emph{disinflation}.

The labor-market dictionary contains terms such as \emph{employment},
\emph{unemployment}, \emph{labor market}, \emph{job gains}, \emph{payrolls},
\emph{hiring}, \emph{layoffs}, \emph{wages}, \emph{labor demand}, \emph{labor supply},
\emph{labor force}, \emph{slack}, \emph{participation}, \emph{vacancies}, and
\emph{job openings}.

For a document $d$, the two attention scores are:

\begin{equation}
    I(d) = 1000 \times
    \frac{\#\{\text{inflation terms in } d\}}
    {\#\{\text{words in } d\}},
    \qquad
    L(d) = 1000 \times
    \frac{\#\{\text{labor-market terms in } d\}}
    {\#\{\text{words in } d\}}.
\end{equation}

The balance score is:

\begin{equation}
    B(d) = I(d) - L(d).
    \label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}

A positive value means that inflation terms are more frequent than labor-market terms.
The matching is intentionally transparent. It also has limits: phrases can overlap with
single words, and words such as \emph{wages} can appear in both labor-market and
inflation-pressure contexts.

\subsection{Dictionary checks}

I add two simple checks because the dictionary is the core measurement choice.

The first check counts which terms drive the score. A dictionary measure can look stable
while being driven by one repeated word, so the term composition matters. The second
check works at the sentence level. Each sentence is tagged as \emph{inflation only},
\emph{labor only}, \emph{both}, or \emph{neither}, depending on which dictionary terms
it contains.
This gives a sentence-level check on whether the document score is supported by
identifiable sentences.

\subsection{\tfidf{} geometry}

As a final document-level check, I use a low-rank \tfidf{} representation. After vectorizing
statements and minutes with unigrams and bigrams, I apply truncated SVD:

\begin{equation}
    X \approx U_k \Sigma_k V_k^\top .
\end{equation}

This is the usual latent semantic analysis view \citep{deerwester1990indexing}. If
statements and minutes separate strongly in this space, then document format is an
important source of variation and should be kept in mind when reading the dictionary
results.

\section{Sentence-Level Topic Classification}
\label{sec:sentence-classifier}

The dictionary is readable and easy to inspect, but it sees exact terms. This matters in
\fomc{} texts. A sentence about credit conditions, market liquidity, or financial stress
can be central to the Fed's communication while using none of the inflation or
labor-market words above. The global financial crisis is the clearest example: much of
the relevant language is about markets, credit, and liquidity, which sit outside the two
mandate dictionaries.

To check this more directly, I split the statements and minutes into 58,976 sentences.
For each sentence, I keep the meeting date, document type, sentence position, rate
decision, period, election-window indicators, and dictionary label. I then draw a
balanced sample of 400 sentences. The sample includes statements and
minutes, inflation-only sentences, labor-only sentences, sentences containing both
dictionaries, sentences containing neither dictionary, meetings with large
statement--minutes gaps, crisis and post-Covid inflation periods, and federal election
windows. This sampling design covers the easy dictionary cases and the harder sentences
where exact matching is likely to miss something.

\subsection{Automatic sentence labels}

The 400 sampled sentences are labelled with a local language model using a fixed JSON
schema. The main topic can be \emph{inflation}, \emph{labor}, \emph{both},
\emph{financial conditions}, \emph{policy decision}, or \emph{other}. The prompt asks
the model to base the classification on the sentence itself and to use \emph{financial
conditions} for sentences about credit, markets, liquidity, volatility, asset prices, or
market functioning. These labels are pseudo-labels: they provide a second, broader
reading of the same sentences and a training target for a small classifier.

In this balanced sample, the most striking result is how often the automatic label is
\emph{financial conditions}: 34.0\% of the sample. Because the sample is balanced and
tilted toward hard cases, this percentage is best read as a stress test of the
dictionary. It reveals an important category of sentences outside the inflation--labor
dictionary. In the pseudo-labels, many dictionary-\emph{neither} sentences contain
substantive background on credit, market functioning, or financial stress.

\reportfigure{figures/dictionary_llm_sentence_confusion.png}{0.82}
{Dictionary sentence labels compared with pseudo-labelled sentence topics. The main gap is that many dictionary-\emph{other} sentences are labelled as financial conditions.}

The comparison is reassuring in the easy cases. Sentences with explicit inflation words
are mostly labelled as inflation or both. Sentences with explicit labor-market words are
mostly labelled as labor or both. But the disagreement is informative. Financial
conditions appear across all dictionary buckets, especially in sentences that the
dictionary calls \emph{other}. This helps explain why the global financial crisis needs
more than an inflation-minus-labor score: the central topic in many sentences is a third
category.

\subsection{Dictionary, \tfidf{}, and embeddings}

I then train small sentence classifiers on the pseudo-labels. This is the main ML/NLP
addition to the report. The question is direct: can the sentence topics be learned from
text, and do embeddings add information beyond exact dictionary matching?
The target is the main topic, with \emph{other} and \emph{policy decision} merged into a
single non-mandate category when the class sizes are too small.

I compare three models. The first is the dictionary rule itself. It is transparent and
uses sentences that contain inflation or labor terms. The second is a \tfidf{}
logistic-regression classifier with unigrams and bigrams.
This tests whether a standard sparse text representation can learn the pseudo-labels. The
third is a logistic-regression classifier on local sentence embeddings from
\code{nomic-embed-text}. This tests whether semantic features capture nearby language,
for example market stress or liquidity language, outside the dictionary. I also
include a no-training embedding baseline: each sentence embedding is compared with short
prototype descriptions of inflation, labor, financial conditions, policy decisions, and
other text.

\reportfigure{figures/embedding_prototype_confusion.png}{0.66}
{Embedding prototype classifier on the pseudo-labelled sentence sample. This is a no-training baseline: each sentence is assigned to the nearest topic prototype.}

\reportfigure{figures/sentence_classifier_scores.png}{0.72}
{Sentence-level classifier scores. Macro-F1 is the main metric because the pseudo-labels are unevenly distributed.}

\reportfigure{figures/sentence_classifier_confusion_embedding.png}{0.66}
{Confusion matrix for the embedding logistic-regression sentence classifier.}

The results show a clear ordering. The dictionary rule reaches macro-F1 0.526 on the
pseudo-labelled sample. The \tfidf{} logistic classifier reaches 0.724, and the
embedding logistic classifier reaches 0.765. Accuracy follows the same pattern:
0.535, 0.718, and 0.768. These scores measure agreement with the pseudo-labels. The
comparison is informative: sentence topics extend beyond exact dictionary hits. There is
recoverable semantic structure around inflation, labor, financial conditions, and
policy-decision language.

\subsection{Applying the classifier to the corpus}

After fitting the selected sentence classifier, I apply it to the full sentence corpus.
When full sentence embeddings are available, I use the embedding classifier. Otherwise,
the notebook falls back to the \tfidf{} classifier. In the results reported here, full
embeddings are available and the full corpus is classified with the embedding logistic
model. The resulting file
\code{data/processed/fomc\_sentence\_topic\_predictions.csv} keeps the predicted topic,
prediction confidence, date, year, document type, period, and election-window variables
for each sentence.

\reportfigure{figures/embedding_topic_shares_over_time.png}{0.82}

This full-corpus step is the bridge between the old score and the new classifier. The
dictionary balance remains important because it has a direct interpretation. The
classifier adds a second view: whether the same meeting looks inflation-heavy or
labor-heavy after sentences are classified by broader semantic features. In the full
corpus, the classifier predicts a large financial-conditions share, especially in
minutes: 49.4\% of minutes sentences against 24.6\% of statement sentences. Statements
have more sentences that combine both sides of the mandate, 17.9\% against 5.0\% in
minutes. These levels partly reflect the pseudo-labels and the balanced training sample,
so the strongest reading is comparative: statements compress the mandate language, while
minutes contain more financial-conditions sentences and longer background discussion.

differences are small: the predicted topic shares inside and outside 90-day election
windows are almost identical, and the dictionary balance changes little.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The main result is simple. Statements and minutes allocate attention differently.
Statements are short and more inflation-dominant. Minutes are longer, more diffuse, and
closer to balance. The statement--minutes gap is itself informative because it shows how
much the immediate policy message differs from the longer meeting account.

This result is expected, and still informative. Inflation is the part of the dual mandate
most directly tied to the Fed's credibility and to expectations. That makes it natural
for statements to put inflation language in the foreground, especially when the Committee
is explaining the policy decision. Labor-market language is still present, but it often
works differently: it helps describe slack, demand, wages, and the cost of tightening.
The two sides of the mandate are communicated through different textual roles.

The dictionary checks make the measure easier to trust, but they also show its limits.
The term \emph{inflation} matters a lot, and some terms are ambiguous. More importantly,
the sentence labels show that some important sentences belong to a third category.
Financial conditions, credit, market functioning, and liquidity form a separate topic in
many crisis-period sentences. The balance score is therefore best read as a descriptive
measure of inflation-versus-labor attention, while the sentence classifier adds the
financial-conditions dimension.

The rate-decision and period figures show that the balance moves in plausible
directions. Hike meetings are more inflation-heavy than cut meetings, and the Covid shock
is one of the clearest points where labor-market language becomes more visible. These are
expected patterns, but seeing them in a simple text measure is informative. The figures
describe associations in a small sample, with some periods containing few meetings.

The low-rank \tfidf{} map gives a methodological lesson. Document format matters a lot.
Models that pool statements and minutes can mainly learn the difference between a short
public announcement and a long meeting summary. The sentence classifier addresses a
different problem: it asks whether topic labels are learnable from sentence text and
whether embeddings add something beyond exact dictionary terms. That makes embeddings a
substantive part of the report.

\section{Connection with the Companion Report}
\label{sec:companion}

This report and the companion hawkish/dovish report share the same corpus, but they
measure different objects. The mandate balance asks what macro topic is emphasized:
inflation or the labor market. The hawkish/dovish score asks what policy signal the text
sends. Keeping the two dimensions separate avoids treating all inflation language as
hawkish and all labor-market language as dovish.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this sample of 199 \fomc{} meetings, statements and minutes give different textual
weights to the two sides of the dual mandate. Statements are much shorter and more
inflation-dominant. Minutes are more balanced and contain a wider discussion. The
sentence-level checks support this reading while keeping the limits of the dictionary
visible. The sentence classifier adds the main ML/NLP result: inflation, labor,
financial-conditions, and policy-decision sentences are partly learnable from text, and
embeddings provide a way to measure those topics beyond exact keyword matches.

The main finding combines a strong expected pattern with one important qualification.
The expected pattern is that inflation language dominates, especially in statements. The
qualification is that the inflation--labor contrast covers one part of the
communication problem. In stress periods, financial conditions become central, and a
pure dual-mandate dictionary misses that part of the story. For this report, the
practical result is a readable pipeline: start with a transparent balance score, inspect
the sentences behind it, use sentence pseudo-labels to train small classifiers, and
compare the dictionary view with a broader sentence-topic view.

Before the sentence-level model, I also tried a small local embedding check with
\code{nomic-embed-text}. Each document is represented by a short beginning/middle/end
excerpt, and the document vector is compared with one inflation prototype phrase and one
labor-market prototype phrase. This document-level view is noisier than the sentence
classifier, especially for minutes, so I keep it in the appendix. It points to the same
message as the LSA map: statements and minutes are different document formats. The main
embedding result in the report is now the sentence-level classifier in
